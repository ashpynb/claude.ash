# Comedy Techniques

## Subverted Expectations

The core of almost all humor. You establish a pattern or direction, then break it in a way that's surprising but feels correct.

**Structure:** Setup implies direction A → punchline delivers direction B (which, on reflection, was always there)

**Bad:** "My dog is so lazy he doesn't fetch the ball." *(too expected)*
**Better:** "My dog has boundary issues. He'll watch you throw the ball, make eye contact, and then walk away like you embarrassed him."

The turn must feel *inevitable in retrospect* — not random. Random is just confusing.

**Test:** After you write the punchline, ask: "does this feel like the only thing it could have been?" If not, keep digging.

## Specificity

The most reliable upgrade to any joke. Replace generic with specific, visual, and particular.

**Levels of specificity:**
- Generic: "a car"
- Better: "a SUV"
- Better: "a Volvo"
- Best: "a Volvo with a stick-family decal and one of the kids crossed out"

**Why it works:** Specific details trigger recognition. They also signal confidence — you know exactly what you're talking about, which gives the audience permission to trust you.

**Practice:** Take your draft. Find every noun. Ask: what's the *most specific version* of this thing that still serves the joke?

## Rule of Three

Establishes a pattern with two examples, then breaks it with the third. The third element should be in the same category but deliver the subversion.

**Classic structure:**
> "I like long walks, meaningful conversation, and [absurd/dark/unexpected third thing]."

**Example:**
> "She wanted someone patient, kind, and available on federal holidays — apparently that rules out every man I've ever dated and also the post office."

The third element should feel like it belongs in the category while exploding the expectation.

**Variation — escalating three:** Each element escalates in intensity, with the third being the peak:
> "It's fine. It's fine. It's absolutely fine." *(the repetition itself becomes the subversion)*

## The Callback

Reference something from earlier in the set/piece, in a new context, usually at the end. Rewards the audience for paying attention and makes the set feel like a unified structure rather than a list of jokes.

**How to plant one:**
1. Say something specific early that seems throwaway
2. 3–10 beats later, reference it again in a completely different context
3. The callback gets a bigger laugh than the original line because of the payoff

**Example:**
> *(Earlier: "My therapist told me I have trouble with vulnerability.")*
> *(Later: "So I tried being vulnerable. I cried in a Panera. My therapist said that wasn't what she meant.")*

Don't announce the callback. Just do it.

## Status

Comedy frequently involves status — high-status people brought low, low-status people unexpectedly elevated, or the gap between claimed status and actual status.

**Status reversal:** The boss who is terrified of their intern. The dog who clearly runs the household.

**Status gap:** Someone presenting as high-status while being obviously low-status. The LinkedIn post written like a Navy SEAL memoir about leading a Q3 synergy initiative.

**Self-deprecation:** Works because the speaker voluntarily lowers their own status. Fails when it becomes self-pity (which asks the audience to comfort you).

**Punching:** Punch at institutions, systems, and people with power. Be careful punching at people without power — it often just reads as cruelty.

## Misdirection

Lead the audience down one interpretive path, then reveal they were on a different one the whole time.

**Common patterns:**
- Ambiguous pronoun that resolves unexpectedly
- Setup that implies one genre/context, punchline that reveals another
- Description that fits two things — the expected thing and the actual thing

**Example:**
> "My father cried at my wedding. Which was unexpected — usually he just sits there and waits for the open bar."
*(The setup implies emotion; the punchline reveals a different, more specific truth.)*

**Key:** The misdirection must be planted fairly. The audience should feel like they *could* have seen it — but didn't.

## Rhythm and Timing

Comedy is music. Word placement and sentence length affect where the laugh lands.

**The punchline goes last.** Structure every sentence so the funny word/image is the final one.

- Weak: "Technically, I'm a vegetarian — I just eat meat sometimes."
- Better: "I'm a vegetarian. Philosophically. Meat-wise, I'm a mess."

**Short sentences hit harder.** Long setups with long punchlines diffuse the impact. When in writing (not performance), shorter = punchier.

**The rule of the unnecessary word:** If cutting a word makes the joke land better, cut it. Comedy prefers pruned sentences.

**Pause via punctuation:** In writing, a period where a comma would go creates a beat. A dash creates a beat. Use them to control where the reader breathes.

**Example of rhythm work:**
> Weak: "I had a very difficult childhood that I'm still processing as an adult."
> Better: "I had a complicated childhood. I'm still in the middle of it, apparently."

## Escalation

Start at a true observation, then follow it to increasingly extreme logical conclusions. The comedy is in how seriously you pursue the logic.

**Structure:** True premise → logical extension 1 → logical extension 2 → absurd but internally consistent conclusion

**Example:**
> "I've decided to be more present. No phone at dinner. No phone on walks. No phone during conversations. I brought a notebook instead. I've started taking notes during conversations. My therapist said this is worse."

The key: each step must follow logically from the last. Escalation is not randomness — it's *committed reasoning.*

---

## Truth Underneath

Almost every good joke has an honest observation underneath it. Before writing the joke, find what's *true* about the topic that usually goes unsaid.

**Questions to ask:**
- What does everyone secretly think about this but never say?
- What's the absurd implication of how we normally talk about this?
- What's the gap between how this is presented and how it actually is?

That gap — between the official version and the real version — is where most comedy lives.
