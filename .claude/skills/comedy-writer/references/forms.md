# Comedy Forms

## Stand-Up Bit

**What it is:** A structured comedic unit with a premise, development, and payoff. Can be 30 seconds or 5 minutes.

**Core structure:**

```
PREMISE: The true observation or situation
ANGLE: Your specific take on it (not generic — *your* take)
FIRST EXAMPLE: Specific. Visual. Escalates slightly.
SECOND EXAMPLE: More specific. Escalates further.
THIRD EXAMPLE / PUNCHLINE: The turn. Or the callback. Or the hardest subversion.
TAG: Optional — a quick addition after the punchline that extends or twists further.
```

**Example skeleton:**
> "I've been thinking about [specific thing]. The thing nobody talks about is [honest observation].
> Like when [specific example — visual and particular].
> Or [second example — escalates].
> My [person] actually [third example — furthest turn].
> [Tag: one more]"

**Tips:**
- The premise should be specific enough that it feels *yours*, not everyone's
- Each example should be more specific than the last
- Never explain what you just said — the tag should extend, not clarify
- Write how you speak, not how you write

## One-Liner

**What it is:** One premise, one turn, maximum compression. Usually one or two sentences.

**Structure:** Setup (establishes expectation) + punchline (subverts it, lands on funny word last)

**Tight construction rules:**
1. Cut every word that doesn't serve the joke
2. The funny word/image is the last one
3. If it takes more than two beats to get to the turn, tighten the setup
4. Test by reading aloud: does it land where you intended?

**Examples of structure:**

*Status reversal one-liner:*
> "I'm not unemployed. I'm self-directed, with results pending."

*Misdirection one-liner:*
> "I've started journaling. It's mostly grievances."

*Escalation compressed to one line:*
> "I don't have commitment issues — I just prefer to see how things go, which is why I've been 'seeing how things go' with the same therapist for eleven years."

**Common mistakes:**
- Setup is too long relative to the punchline
- Punchline word isn't last
- Two premises competing in the same joke

## Monologue

**What it is:** First-person confessional or observational. A sustained comic voice builds a situation, lets it spiral, and ends on a note that's specific and a little self-defeating.

**Structure:**
```
OPENING: A declaration or confession that signals the speaker's worldview
BUILD: Accumulate details that make the situation increasingly specific and absurd
ESCALATE: Follow the logic wherever it goes — further than is comfortable
LAND: End on something mundane, self-aware, or quietly dark — not a big swing
```

**Voice is everything in monologue.** The funniness comes from *who is speaking* as much as what's being said. The speaker should have:
- A consistent, slightly wrong worldview
- Confidence in their position even as it deteriorates
- A specific relationship to their own intelligence/competence

**Example of landing:**
> *(After building a detailed case for why you're fine)* "...So. I'm doing great. I made a spreadsheet."

**Tips:**
- The ending should feel earned, not punchy — monologue doesn't need a punchline so much as a deflation
- The voice stays consistent even as the situation gets worse
- Digressions that pay off later are better than straight lines

## Observational Story

**What it is:** "Have you ever noticed..." extended into a story with characters, escalating stakes, and an earned conclusion.

**Structure:**
```
UNIVERSAL HOOK: An experience everyone recognizes
SPECIFIC VERSION: Your particular version of it, with specific details
CHARACTERS: Give them specific traits that serve the joke
ESCALATION: The situation gets more absurd while staying internally consistent
TRUTH: The conclusion reveals something honest about people/life
```

**Key:** The story needs to actually go somewhere. The ending should recontextualize the beginning, not just stop.

**Example of recontextualization:**
> *(A story about trying to cancel a subscription that ends with the revelation that you've been talking to a human named Steve who just sounded like a robot — the story about automation becomes a story about alienation.)*

**Tips:**
- Characters need one specific, memorable detail — not full bios
- Let the escalation get uncomfortable before releasing it
- The funniest part is usually the most specific, most true thing — bury it in the middle, return to it at the end

## Roast

**What it is:** Comedy targeted at a specific person, usually with affection underneath. Specific observations about the target, exaggerated to their logical extreme.

**Structure:**
```
TRUE OBSERVATION about the target
EXAGGERATION of that observation
AFFECTIONATE/DEVASTATING punchline that confirms the observation
```

**What makes roast work:**
- **Specificity to the target.** Generic insults are just mean. Specific observations that are recognizably *true about that person* are funny.
- **Affection or respect underneath.** Roast that's only hostile reads as an attack. The audience needs to feel the love in the exaggeration.
- **Truth first.** The observation has to be accurate before it can be funny.

**What kills roast:**
- Generic shots that could apply to anyone
- Actual malice with no warmth
- Piling on a weakness that's clearly a wound rather than a quirk
- Being vague about what you're actually saying

**Example structure:**
> "[Person] is [true observation]. The kind of [true observation] that [specific, visual instance]. I've never seen someone [thing] with such [quality]. It's inspiring. It's almost [unexpected adjective]. My [relationship] does the same thing, but [comparison that brings it back to the target]."

## Comic Essay

**What it is:** Long-form comedy with a consistent voice, a thesis that gets weirder as it's proven, and structural rewards for readers who stay.

**Structure:**
```
THESIS: A specific, slightly unusual position stated plainly
PROOF 1: First argument — reasonable, establishes the voice
PROOF 2: Second argument — more specific, shows commitment
DIGRESSION: A tangent that seems unrelated but will pay off
PROOF 3: Most extreme argument — internal logic fully committed
CALLBACK TO DIGRESSION: Now it connects
CONCLUSION: The thesis, restated with its full implications acknowledged
```

**Voice markers for comic essay:**
- Treats absurd things with total seriousness
- Uses citations, analogies, or data to prove something that doesn't require proof
- Footnotes can be load-bearing (a technique for hiding the funniest line)
- The essay's structure itself can become part of the joke

**Tonal guide:**
- Commit to the voice even when the subject matter is objectively silly
- The comedy comes from the gap between the serious apparatus and the ridiculous subject
- Never break the fourth wall — the essayist believes this

**Examples of thesis:**
- "The thank-you email after a networking call is the most dishonest document in business communication."
- "Dogs are not loyal. They're just on a longer con."
- "There is no correct amount of sauce. This is the central lie of all recipe writing."
